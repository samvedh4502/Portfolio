NREL S826 Aerofoil CFD Validation & Wind-Tunnel Correlation

STAR-CCM+ CFD and wind-tunnel evaluation of an NREL S826 aerofoil across increasing angle of attack. The project combined mesh convergence, low-y+ wall treatment, pressure and velocity field interpretation, and CFD-to-experiment correlation for lift coefficient, drag coefficient and surface pressure distribution.

Project summary

What this project demonstrates

This project demonstrates a complete validation workflow for an aerofoil: CFD model preparation, mesh-convergence assessment, wall-y+ strategy comparison, physical wind-tunnel testing, and correlation between numerical and experimental aerodynamic data. The work focused on understanding not just the final coefficient values, but also why correlation quality changed with angle of attack.

Engineering question

How accurately can a STAR-CCM+ RANS CFD model predict the aerodynamic performance of an NREL S826 aerofoil when compared against wind-tunnel measurements across increasing angle of attack?

01 — Experimental setup

Wind-Tunnel Testing Workflow

The aerofoil was tested in the Oxford Brookes wind tunnel using a controlled angle-of-attack sweep. The workflow involved securing the model, connecting pressure tappings to the pressure scanner, aligning the Pitot-static tube, setting the micromanometer to read velocity, entering ambient conditions into VDAS, and logging the data for each angle.

Experimental slide 01

Wind-tunnel rig

The full tunnel setup provided the physical test environment for the aerofoil correlation study. Using an experimental rig made the project more than a numerical exercise: the CFD outputs could be checked against measured wind-tunnel behaviour.

Experimental slide 02

Aerofoil mounted in working section

The mounted aerofoil was connected through pressure tappings so that surface pressure distribution could be measured and compared against CFD pressure-coefficient predictions at multiple angles of attack.

Experimental slide 03

Micromanometer and pressure measurement

The micromanometer and pressure-scanning system were used to support tunnel-speed and pressure measurement. This instrumentation linked the physical test to the coefficient and pressure-correlation plots used later in the analysis.

02 — CFD methodology

Mesh Convergence and Wall-y+ Strategy

The CFD setup used STAR-CCM+ with 2D aerofoil meshing, local surface refinement, trailing-edge refinement, wake refinement and prism-layer control. Mesh convergence and wall-y+ treatment were assessed before using the CFD data for experimental correlation.

CFD slide 01

Mesh convergence

Mesh convergence was assessed at 16° angle of attack. The selected 4 mm base-size mesh gave the best balance between accuracy and computational cost, while coarser meshes showed larger coefficient error and weaker correlation reliability.

CFD slide 02

Prism layers and wake refinement

Local mesh refinement was applied around the aerofoil surface, leading edge, trailing edge and wake. This was necessary because lift, drag and pressure recovery are sensitive to near-wall gradients, separation behaviour and wake momentum deficit.

CFD slide 03

Low-y+ wall treatment

The low-y+ approach gave better agreement than the high-y+ approach. This was important because aerofoil performance is dominated by near-wall behaviour, leading-edge suction and separation sensitivity, especially as angle of attack increases.

03 — Flow-field analysis

Velocity Field Development with Angle of Attack

The velocity contours were used to interpret wake development and increasing separation tendency as angle of attack increased. This helped explain why low-angle cases correlated better than higher-angle cases.

Flow-field slide 01

0° angle of attack

At low angle of attack, the flow field remains comparatively attached and the wake is narrow. This condition is easier for steady RANS CFD to predict, which supports stronger experimental correlation at lower angles.

Flow-field slide 02

8° angle of attack

At moderate angle of attack, the suction-side acceleration and wake structure become more pronounced. The pressure recovery region becomes more sensitive to mesh resolution and turbulence modelling.

Flow-field slide 03

16° angle of attack

At high angle of attack, the wake becomes stronger and separation sensitivity increases. This is where the CFD-to-experiment correlation becomes more difficult because the result is more dependent on turbulence modelling, near-wall resolution and experimental uncertainty.

04 — Correlation results

CFD versus Wind-Tunnel Correlation

The correlation work compared global force coefficients and surface pressure distribution. The coefficient comparison showed the overall performance trend, while the Cp plots showed how well CFD captured the pressure distribution along the aerofoil chord at each angle of attack.

Correlation slide 01

Lift coefficient correlation

The CFD and experimental lift trends showed reasonable agreement, with an average Cl error of 10.08%. Agreement was strongest at lower angles of attack and weakened as separation effects became more dominant.

Correlation slide 02

Cp correlation at 0°

At 0°, the pressure distribution remains comparatively stable and attached. This case is useful as a low-angle baseline for checking whether the CFD captures the basic suction and pressure-side behaviour.

Correlation slide 03

Cp correlation at 4°

At 4°, the pressure difference between the upper and lower surfaces increases, but the flow is still relatively controlled. This allows the CFD model to capture the trend without the strong separation uncertainty seen at higher angles.

Correlation slide 04

Cp correlation at 8°

At 8°, suction-side loading becomes stronger and the CFD prediction becomes more sensitive to the leading-edge pressure peak, pressure recovery and near-wall model behaviour.

Correlation slide 05

Cp correlation at 12°

At 12°, adverse pressure-gradient effects become more important. The upper-surface pressure recovery is harder to predict accurately, so deviations between CFD and wind-tunnel measurements become more visible.

Correlation slide 06

Cp correlation at 16°

At 16°, separation and stall-onset sensitivity make the correlation most difficult. This explains why the error increases at higher angle of attack: the flow is no longer dominated by attached-flow behaviour.

05 — Results

Engineering Outcomes

CFD validation workflow

The project connected mesh convergence, near-wall modelling and CFD post-processing with experimental wind-tunnel data rather than treating CFD as a standalone visualisation exercise.

Coefficient correlation

The CFD model achieved an average error of 10.08% for Cl and 15.61% for Cd when compared with the experimental wind-tunnel results.

Pressure-distribution comparison

Cp correlation was carried out at multiple angles of attack, allowing the comparison to move beyond global coefficient values and evaluate chordwise surface-pressure behaviour.

High-AoA interpretation

Correlation error increased at higher angle of attack because stronger adverse pressure gradients and separation tendency made the CFD result more sensitive to turbulence modelling and experimental uncertainty.

06 — Reflection

Limitations & Next Steps

Limitations

  • Steady RANS CFD was used, so transient separated-flow behaviour was not fully resolved.
  • Transition modelling was not treated as a dedicated sensitivity study.
  • Experimental uncertainty from pressure tappings, model alignment and tunnel conditions may affect correlation.
  • The aerofoil was studied primarily as a 2D section, so finite-span and support effects were not fully represented.

Future work

  • Run transition-sensitive simulations using a dedicated transition model.
  • Compare steady RANS with URANS at higher angle of attack.
  • Add finer leading-edge and wake refinement for separated-flow cases.
  • Quantify uncertainty in pressure tapping data and angle-of-attack alignment.
  • Extend the study to a 3D wing section with endplate and support effects included.
